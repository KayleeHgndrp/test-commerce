There are four attachment styles: secure, anxious, avoidant and fearful-avoidant. They describe how you tend to act when you want closeness, fear losing it or feel crowded by it. Your style is decided by two things: how much you worry about being rejected, and how comfortable you are with depending on someone.

That sounds abstract, so this article keeps it concrete. You will read where the styles come from, what each one looks like on an ordinary day, three situations in which the four styles react very differently, and what actually helps if you recognise yourself in one of the insecure styles.

## Where attachment styles come from

Attachment theory started with the British psychiatrist John Bowlby. In the 1950s and 1960s he argued that babies are born with a system that keeps them close to the people who care for them. When a child feels unsafe, the system switches on and the child looks for comfort. When comfort comes, the system calms down again.

The psychologist Mary Ainsworth turned that idea into research. In a setup known as the Strange Situation, she watched how one year olds reacted when their parent briefly left the room and came back. Some children were upset and then quickly comforted. Some barely seemed to react. Others stayed upset and angry, even in their parent's arms. Later, Mary Main and Judith Solomon added a fourth group: children whose behaviour seemed confused, as if they wanted to approach the parent and move away at the same time.

In 1987 Cindy Hazan and Phillip Shaver showed that adults bring similar patterns into their love lives. A few years later Kim Bartholomew and Leonard Horowitz described the four adult styles that most people know today. Since then, researchers usually measure attachment with two scales instead of fixed boxes.

One important point before we go on: your style is not a verdict on your childhood. Early experiences play a role, but so do later relationships, friendships, losses and plain temperament.

## Two scales, four styles

The first scale is **attachment anxiety**. It measures how much you worry about being left, rejected or loved less than you love. The second is **attachment avoidance**. It measures how uneasy you feel with closeness and with relying on someone else.

Put those two scales together and you get four combinations:

| | Low avoidance | High avoidance |
|---|---|---|
| **Low anxiety** | Secure | Dismissive-avoidant |
| **High anxiety** | Anxious-preoccupied | Fearful-avoidant |

Because these are scales, nobody is purely one style. Two people with an anxious style can be very different: one is a bit anxious in a new relationship, the other worries all the time. You may also sit close to the line between two styles. That is why a description of a style is always a sketch, not a portrait.

## The four styles up close

### Secure attachment

People with a secure style mostly trust that the people they love care about them. They can be close without feeling trapped, and alone without feeling abandoned. When their partner goes on a trip with friends, they miss them and also enjoy the free evening.

Secure does not mean perfect. Secure people get jealous, sulk after an argument and have bad days. The difference is that they recover faster and find it easier to say what is going on. "I felt left out yesterday, can we talk about it?" is a very secure sentence.

### Anxious attachment

With an anxious style, also called anxious-preoccupied, closeness matters a lot and feels easy to lose. Your attention goes to small signals: a shorter message, a different tone, a partner who seems distracted. When you sense distance, you want to restore contact quickly.

Anxious people are often warm, loyal and very aware of how others feel. The hard part is that their sense of calm depends heavily on the other person. A late reply can ruin an evening. We wrote a separate article on that exact moment: [why a late reply hits so hard with anxious attachment](/en/blog/anxious-attachment-texting).

### Avoidant attachment

With an avoidant style, also called dismissive-avoidant, independence feels safe and too much closeness feels like pressure. Avoidant people often seem calm, capable and self-sufficient. They tend to solve problems alone and feel uneasy when someone leans on them emotionally.

This does not mean avoidant people do not care. Research suggests they feel stress in relationships too, but they push it down or redirect it into practical things. When a conversation gets emotional, an avoidant person may suddenly start talking about logistics, change the subject or need to go for a run.

### Fearful-avoidant attachment

The fearful-avoidant style combines high anxiety with high avoidance. You want closeness and you are afraid of it. When someone comes near, part of you relaxes and part of you braces for disappointment. When someone pulls away, you feel the loss strongly, yet you may also feel relieved.

This can make relationships feel like a rollercoaster, for yourself and for the people around you. One week you share a lot, the next you need distance and cannot explain why. Fearful-avoidant attachment is often linked to experiences in which the people who should give comfort were also a source of stress. That is not always the case, and it is never a reason for shame.

## Three everyday moments, four reactions

Styles become clearer when you see them in action. Here are three ordinary situations and how each style might react. You will probably recognise yourself in more than one, which is normal.

### Your partner cancels Friday night

It is Friday at five. Your partner texts: "Sorry, totally wiped out, can we move dinner to Sunday?"

The **secure** person feels a flash of disappointment, replies "Shame, but fine. Rest well" and orders a pizza. The **anxious** person reads the message three times. Was it really tiredness? Is something wrong? They send a second message asking if everything is okay and keep checking the phone all evening. The **avoidant** person feels mostly relief at a free evening and replies with a thumbs up. They may not notice that their partner hoped for a warmer reaction. The **fearful-avoidant** person first feels hurt, then decides they did not want to go anyway, and then spends the evening wondering if this is the beginning of the end.

### An argument about the dishes

It is not really about the dishes. It is about feeling that you do more than your share. Voices get a little louder.

The **secure** person stays in the conversation, even when it is unpleasant, and might say: "I am getting annoyed, can we take five minutes?" The **anxious** person wants to solve it now. A break feels like abandonment, so they follow their partner into the next room. The **avoidant** person shuts down. They go quiet, look at their phone or leave the house to "clear their head". To them this feels like keeping the peace. The **fearful-avoidant** person may swing between both: first pushing for a solution, then suddenly saying "forget it, it does not matter" and withdrawing completely.

### The third date goes really well

You have met someone. The third date is lovely, you talk until midnight, and they say: "I really like you."

The **secure** person feels happy and a little nervous and looks forward to the next date. The **anxious** person feels a rush of joy that quickly turns into tension. Now there is something to lose. They start thinking about how to keep it. The **avoidant** person likes the other person too, but the sentence lands as pressure. The next few days they answer a bit slower and plan less. The **fearful-avoidant** person feels exactly that pull and push at once. They might text something very warm late at night and then not reply for a day.

In all three moments, the facts are small. The difference is in what each style expects to happen next.

## Which attachment style do I have?

Most people can make a first guess by looking at their own reactions in stressful moments, not in calm ones. When things are good, almost everyone looks secure. Try these questions:

- When someone you love is distant for a day, do you mostly feel calm, worried, relieved or a mix of worried and relieved?
- After an argument, do you want to talk it out right away, have time alone first, or both at once?
- How do you feel when a partner says they need you? Warm, proud, uneasy or trapped?
- Do you find it easier to give support or to receive it?
- Do your relationships tend to feel steady, intense, distant or unpredictable?

Also look at your patterns over time. Do you notice that you lose interest exactly when someone becomes available? Do you often feel you care more than the other person? Do friends describe you as independent, needy or hard to read? Those observations say more than one bad week.

If worry is a big part of your answer, you may also recognise yourself in our article on [the signs that you are an overthinker](/en/blog/signs-you-are-an-overthinker). Anxious attachment and overthinking often travel together, especially at night.

## Attachment styles outside of love: friends and work

Attachment styles are usually discussed in the context of dating, but the same two scales show up wherever you depend on someone. Friendships and work are the most common places.

With friends, anxious attachment often looks like keeping score of who texts first, or feeling left out when you see a group photo you were not in. Avoidant attachment looks like being great company and then disappearing for weeks when life gets busy, without noticing that others miss you.

At work the patterns are quieter. Someone with an anxious style may read a short email from a manager as a sign of trouble and spend the evening going over it. If that sounds familiar, our article on [why small criticism can hit so hard](/en/blog/rejection-sensitivity-criticism) looks at that reaction in detail. Many anxious people also say yes to every extra task, because a no feels like a risk to the relationship. [Saying no without guilt](/en/blog/how-to-say-no-without-guilt) is a skill you can practise, and it gets easier with every small try. Avoidant colleagues tend to do the opposite: they would rather solve everything alone than ask for help.

## How styles meet in a relationship

Your style does not exist on its own. It shows up most clearly in combination with someone else's.

Two secure partners usually give each other space and closeness without much effort. A secure partner can also help an insecure partner feel calmer over time, simply by being predictable and kind.

The best known combination is **anxious and avoidant**. The anxious partner seeks contact, the avoidant partner feels crowded and pulls back, and that makes the anxious partner reach out even harder. Both are trying to feel safe, but in opposite ways. Many couples stay stuck in this loop for years without realising it is a pattern rather than a personality clash.

Two anxious partners understand each other's need for closeness, but they can also feed each other's worries. Two avoidant partners rarely crowd each other, but important feelings can stay unspoken until the relationship feels more like a flatshare. A fearful-avoidant partner can find any combination intense, because the wish for closeness and the urge to escape keep taking turns.

None of these combinations is doomed. What helps is recognising the pattern together and naming it in a calm moment: "When you go quiet, I start chasing you. When I chase you, you go quieter. Shall we try something else?"

## Can your attachment style change?

Yes. Attachment styles are fairly stable, but they are not fixed. Researchers use the term **earned security** for people who grew up with insecure attachment and became more secure later in life, through relationships, friendships, therapy or their own reflection.

Change usually happens in small steps. These help most people:

1. **Notice the moment your system switches on.** A racing heart when a message stays unanswered, or a strong urge to leave when someone gets close. Naming it ("this is my attachment alarm") gives you a little distance.
2. **Pause before you act.** For anxious styles that means waiting before you send the second message. For avoidant styles it means staying in the room for five more minutes before you leave.
3. **Say what you need in plain words.** "It helps me when you text if you are running late" works better than testing whether someone notices.
4. **Practise the opposite move in small doses.** If you are anxious, plan an evening for yourself and enjoy it. If you are avoidant, share one small feeling a day.
5. **Spend time with secure people.** Friends, colleagues or a partner who are steady and honest show your nervous system that closeness can be safe.
6. **Get support when the pattern is heavy.** If relationships keep hurting you, or if fear and distrust run your life, a psychologist or therapist can help more than any article.

Be patient with yourself. A pattern that helped you feel safe for years does not disappear in a month. Every time you act a little more securely than you feel, you build a new habit.
