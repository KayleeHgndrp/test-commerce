The Big Five is the more reliable personality test. It measures five traits on a scale, it was built from research data and it predicts real life outcomes better than the MBTI. The MBTI, and the many free 16 personalities tests based on it, gives you a four letter type that is easy to remember and fun to talk about, but it puts people into boxes and often gives a different result when you retake it.

That does not mean one is useful and the other is nonsense. They answer different questions. Below you will read what each test measures, where they overlap, three everyday situations where the difference matters, and how to get more out of whichever result you already have.

## What does the MBTI measure?

The Myers Briggs Type Indicator was developed in the 1940s by Katharine Cook Briggs and her daughter Isabel Briggs Myers. They based it on the ideas of the Swiss psychiatrist Carl Jung, who wrote about psychological types in the 1920s. Neither of them was a trained psychologist. They wanted a practical tool to help people understand each other.

The MBTI looks at four pairs of preferences:

- **Extraversion (E) or Introversion (I):** do you get your energy from people and activity, or from quiet time and your own thoughts?
- **Sensing (S) or Intuition (N):** do you focus on concrete facts and details, or on patterns, ideas and possibilities?
- **Thinking (T) or Feeling (F):** do you decide mainly on logic, or mainly on values and the effect on people?
- **Judging (J) or Perceiving (P):** do you like plans and closure, or do you prefer to keep options open?

From each pair you get one letter, which gives sixteen combinations such as INFP or ESTJ. The official MBTI is a paid questionnaire. Most free 16 types tests online use the same four letters but are separate tests with their own questions and scoring, so they are not exactly the MBTI.

## What does the Big Five measure?

The Big Five came about in a completely different way. Researchers started from language: if a trait matters in how people differ, there will be many words for it. They collected thousands of descriptive words, asked people to rate themselves and others, and looked at which words clustered together. Over decades and in many languages, the same five broad traits kept coming back.

- **Openness:** curiosity, imagination and interest in new ideas and experiences.
- **Conscientiousness:** organisation, self discipline and following through.
- **Extraversion:** energy from social contact, talkativeness and enthusiasm.
- **Agreeableness:** warmth, cooperation and consideration for others.
- **Emotional sensitivity**, often called neuroticism in research: how strongly you react to stress, worry and setbacks.

You do not get a type. You get a score on each of the five scales, usually as a percentage. Two people can both be "fairly extraverted" and still differ by twenty points. If you want to see what that looks like for you, the [Big Five test](/en/big-five) splits each trait into two aspects, so you get a profile on ten facets.

## The biggest difference: boxes or scales

Most human traits, like height, are spread along a curve. A few people are very tall, a few are very short and most are somewhere in the middle. Personality works the same way. Most people are not clearly introverted or clearly extraverted. They are a bit of both, leaning slightly to one side.

A type test has to draw a line somewhere. If the line is at 50 percent, someone at 49 percent is called an introvert and someone at 51 percent an extravert, even though they are almost the same. Meanwhile someone at 51 and someone at 95 get the same letter, even though they are very different. That is the core problem with types: they make small differences look big and big differences look small.

The Big Five avoids this by keeping the scale. It tells you that you are at 58 percent on extraversion, which says more than the letter E. It also tells you where you stand compared to other people, which a type cannot do.

## Which test is more reliable?

Reliability means two things here. Do you get roughly the same result when you take the test again? And does the result tell you something about your actual life?

On the first point, a known weakness of the MBTI is that a sizeable share of people get a different type when they retake it after a few weeks. That follows directly from the boxes problem: if you score near the middle on even one scale, a small shift flips the letter and your whole type changes. Big Five scores also move a little from day to day, but a move from 52 to 48 percent is a small change on a scale, not a new identity.

On the second point, the Big Five is far better researched. It has been studied in relation to health, work, relationships and wellbeing in many countries. A large comparison reported by Scientific American looked at 37 life outcomes and found that a Big Five test predicted them about twice as well as an MBTI style test, and that the type added nothing once the Big Five scores were known.

One clear reason is the missing fifth trait. The MBTI has no scale for emotional sensitivity. Yet how strongly you react to stress is one of the things that shapes your days the most: how you sleep after a hard conversation, how long a mistake keeps bothering you, how quickly you start to worry. If that is familiar, you may recognise yourself in our article on [the signs you are an overthinker](/en/blog/signs-you-are-an-overthinker).

To be fair to the MBTI: its four scales are not random. When researchers look at the underlying scores instead of the letters, they line up quite well with four of the Big Five traits.

## How the four letters and the five traits relate

| MBTI scale | Closest Big Five trait |
|---|---|
| Extraversion or Introversion | Extraversion |
| Sensing or Intuition | Openness (Intuition goes with higher openness) |
| Thinking or Feeling | Agreeableness (Feeling goes with higher agreeableness) |
| Judging or Perceiving | Conscientiousness (Judging goes with higher conscientiousness) |
| No scale | Emotional sensitivity |

So if you know you are an INFJ, you can guess that you probably score lower on extraversion and higher on openness, agreeableness and conscientiousness. What you cannot know is how strong each of those is, or how you deal with stress.

## Three everyday situations where the difference shows

**The team day.** Mark's company asks everyone to do a 16 types test before a team day. Mark gets INTJ and reads that INTJs are strategic loners who dislike small talk. At the coffee machine he starts saying "I'm an INTJ, I don't do chit chat". His colleague Fatima also got INTJ but is happy to talk about weekends. Their Big Five profiles would explain it: Mark scores low on extraversion and on agreeableness, while Fatima is close to the middle on extraversion. Same letters, different people. The type became a label that Mark hides behind, instead of a starting point for a conversation.

**Two INFPs.** Two friends, Emma and Lotte, both turn out as INFP and laugh about being "the same". But Emma lies awake after every small disagreement and checks her phone ten times when a message goes unanswered, while Lotte shrugs and sleeps fine. The 16 types test has no way to show that difference, because it has no scale for emotional sensitivity. A Big Five test would show Emma high and Lotte low on exactly that trait, which explains more about their daily lives than the four shared letters.

**The retake after a rough month.** Sam did a 16 types test in the summer and got ENFP. In November, after a busy and tiring month, Sam takes it again and gets INFJ. Two letters changed and Sam wonders which one is "real". Looking at the percentages, Sam was at 54 percent extraversion in summer and 46 percent now, and close to the middle on Judging and Perceiving too. Nothing fundamental changed. Sam was tired, wanted more quiet and felt more need for structure. On a Big Five scale that would have shown up as two small shifts instead of a different type.

## How do you know which test fits what you want to know?

Ask yourself what you want from the result. That decides which test is useful.

You will probably enjoy a type test more if you want a quick, memorable description to share with friends or a partner, or a common language for a team to talk about how people like to work. Types are easy to talk about, and that is a real advantage. DISC works the same way at work, and our article on [the four DISC colours](/en/blog/disc-personality-types-colors) explains when that kind of tool helps.

A Big Five test fits better if you want to understand yourself more precisely: why some things drain you, where you tend to get stuck, or how you compare with other people. It also fits if you have done several type tests and got different results, or if stress and worry play a big role in your life.

And you can simply do both. Our [16 types test](/en/16-types) also measures how strongly you lean to each side of the four scales, not just which side, so you can see whether a letter is clear or close to the middle.

## Practical steps to get more out of your result

1. **Look at the numbers, not just the label.** A letter based on 51 percent tells you very little. A score of 85 percent tells you a lot. If a scale is near the middle, read both descriptions and see which parts fit.
2. **Find concrete moments.** For each trait, think of one recent situation where it showed. A high score on agreeableness might be the time you said yes to an extra task you did not want. If that sounds familiar, our piece on [how to say no without guilt](/en/blog/how-to-say-no-without-guilt) goes into it.
3. **Ask someone who knows you.** Show your result to a friend or partner and ask what they recognise and what surprises them. We are not always the best judges of ourselves.
4. **Use it to explain, not to excuse.** "I'm an introvert, so I need a quiet hour after a party" is useful. "I'm an introvert, so I never go to parties" turns a tendency into a rule.
5. **Retake it in a few months.** If your scores hardly move, you have a stable picture. If they move a lot, look at what was going on in your life at the time.

## What a personality test can and cannot tell you

An online personality test describes how you see yourself at this moment, based on your answers to a set of statements. That is useful, because how you see yourself says a lot. But it is not an objective measurement like a blood test.

A test can show you patterns you had not put into words, give you language to explain yourself to others, and help you think about what suits you in work and relationships. It cannot tell you who you should be, which job you will be good at, or whether a relationship will work. Traits are tendencies, not limits. People with low extraversion give great talks, and people with low conscientiousness run successful businesses.

Our tests are based on these well known models, but they are short online versions, not the validated questionnaires a psychologist uses in an assessment. Use them as a mirror, not a verdict. If you notice that worry, low mood or stress really get in the way of your daily life, a test is not the right tool. Talking to your doctor or a psychologist is.

If you are curious where you stand, start with the [Big Five test](/en/big-five) for the most precise picture, and do the [16 types test](/en/16-types) afterwards if you want to compare. Seeing your percentages next to your letters often explains more than either result on its own.
