Criticism hurts so much when you are sensitive to rejection because your mind does not hear "this can be better", it hears "they do not like me". Rejection sensitivity is the tendency to expect rejection, to spot it quickly in unclear situations and to react strongly to it. Almost everyone has some of it. For some people it is so strong that one small remark can ruin a whole day.

The good news is that this pattern can be recognised and softened. Below you will read where it comes from, three moments you probably know from your own life, how to see it in yourself and what you can do the next time a comment hits harder than it should.

## What is rejection sensitivity?

The term comes from the American psychologists Geraldine Downey and Scott Feldman. In 1996 they described people who **anxiously expect** rejection, **readily perceive** it and **overreact** to it. Their idea was simple: if you expect to be rejected, you look for signs of it, and if you look for signs, you find them, even where there are none.

Rejection sensitivity is not a disorder and not a diagnosis. It is a trait on a spectrum, like being more or less extraverted. At the low end are people who shrug off a no. At the high end are people for whom an unanswered message or a frown from a colleague feels like a small earthquake.

In our test we look at four sides of it:

- **Anticipating rejection.** You expect a no before you have even asked. You assume people will find you too much, boring or annoying.
- **Perceiving rejection.** You read rejection into neutral things: a short reply, a meeting you were not invited to, a sigh.
- **Reacting strongly.** When it seems to happen, you feel it physically. Your face gets hot, your stomach turns, you replay the moment for hours.
- **Avoiding.** You do not ask, do not apply and do not share your opinion, so nobody can say no.

Most people are not equally high on all four. Someone can be very alert to signs of rejection and still recover quickly. Someone else hardly expects rejection, but falls apart when it does come.

## Why does criticism hurt so much?

This is the question many people type into Google late at night, after a comment that is still going round in their head. There are a few reasons, and they reinforce each other.

**Rejection is painful for everyone.** People are social animals. For most of human history, being pushed out of the group was dangerous. In a well known study from 2003, Naomi Eisenberger, Matthew Lieberman and Kipling Williams had people play a simple ball game on a screen in which the other players suddenly stopped passing to them. In the brain scans, areas became active that also play a role in the distress of physical pain. That does not mean that rejection is the same as a broken arm. It does mean that "it hurts" is more than a figure of speech.

**Criticism touches a story you already believe.** If somewhere deep down you think you are not good enough, every comment seems to confirm it. The feedback is about a slide in your presentation. What you hear is proof of something you were already afraid of.

**Your expectation colours what you see.** Downey and Feldman found that people who expect rejection more quickly see intent in ambiguous behaviour. A colleague who passes you without saying hello is busy, or tired, or did not see you. Your brain picks the explanation that matches your fear.

**The reaction itself creates new problems.** Later research by Downey and colleagues showed how rejection sensitivity in relationships can become a self fulfilling prophecy. Someone who fears being left reacts with anger or withdrawal to small signals, and that reaction puts pressure on the relationship. You then end up with the very rejection you were afraid of.

## Three moments you might recognise

### The review with one point for improvement

You have your annual review. Your manager is positive about almost everything: your reliability, your contact with clients, how you handled a difficult project. At the end comes one remark: "Your reports could be a bit more concise sometimes."

On your way home, you have forgotten the rest. You keep thinking about those reports. Has she been annoyed for months? Do others think so too? By the evening you are wondering whether you are actually good at your job.

What happened: one specific, fairly mild point grew into a judgement about you as a person. The ten positive comments did not count, because they did not match the story in your head. A useful question afterwards is: what did she literally say? Write it down word for word. Usually what remains on paper is much smaller than what you felt.

### The message that just says "ok"

You send your partner a long message about your plans for the weekend. A while later the reply comes: "ok". No emoji, no question, nothing.

Within a minute you have three explanations, and they are all bad. He is irritated. She does not feel like it. Something is wrong and you do not know what. You type a message asking whether everything is all right, delete it, type it again.

In reality your partner was in a meeting and typed the shortest possible answer under the table. This kind of moment is so common that we wrote a separate article about [why a late or short reply affects you so much](/en/blog/anxious-attachment-texting). Rejection sensitivity and anxious attachment often go together, and the same steps help with both.

### The plan you were not part of

On Sunday you see a photo on Instagram. Three friends at a terrace, glasses raised. You were not invited. Nobody told you anything about it.

The sting is immediate. Then the conclusions follow: they do not really like me, they find me tiring, they only invite me out of politeness. You decide not to react in the group chat for a few days, to protect yourself.

Maybe it was a spontaneous plan between three people who live on the same street. Maybe they did forget you, and that is annoying. But your withdrawal makes the distance bigger, not smaller. A light message such as "That looks fun, next time I'd like to come along" is more likely to get you invited than silence.

## How do you know if you are rejection sensitive?

Everyone is sometimes hurt by criticism. The difference lies in how often it happens, how strong it is and how long it lasts. You may recognise yourself in some of the signs below:

- You replay conversations afterwards, looking for what you did wrong.
- You apologise quickly, even when you are not sure you did anything wrong.
- You pay close attention to tone, facial expressions and the length of messages.
- You hold back an opinion or question because it might lead to a no.
- You feel a flash of anger or shame at criticism before you have even thought about it.
- You ask for reassurance often: "Are you sure you are not angry?"
- You give up on something before someone else can turn you down.
- You work very hard to make sure nobody has anything to criticise.

That last point often goes hand in hand with people pleasing. If you say yes to everything so that nobody will be disappointed in you, our article on [saying no without feeling guilty](/en/blog/how-to-say-no-without-guilt) may be useful.

Also look at what happens in your body. For many people rejection sensitivity is not so much a thought as a physical reaction: a racing heart, a lump in the throat, an urge to leave the room. Noticing that reaction is the first step to not following it automatically.

## Where does it come from?

There is no single cause, and it is rarely anyone's fault. Downey and Feldman assumed that rejection sensitivity grows when a child repeatedly experiences that their needs are met with rejection, coldness or harsh criticism. The child learns to be alert, and that alertness stays.

Other experiences can also play a role: being bullied at school, a parent who was unpredictable, a breakup that came out of nowhere, a job where you were constantly judged. Temperament matters too. Some people are simply more sensitive to signals from their surroundings from birth.

There is a clear link with attachment. People with an anxious attachment style often score high on rejection sensitivity, because both are about the same fear: that the other person will turn away. If you want to understand that better, read our article on [the four attachment styles](/en/blog/attachment-styles-explained).

## Is rejection sensitivity the same as RSD?

On social media you often see the abbreviation RSD, which stands for rejection sensitive dysphoria. The term was popularised by the American psychiatrist William Dodson, mainly in connection with ADHD. It describes sudden, very intense emotional pain after rejection or criticism.

RSD is not an official diagnosis and is not included in the major psychiatric manuals. Many claims you see about it online, including impressive percentages, are not well supported by research. Rejection sensitivity, on the other hand, is a research term that has been studied for decades. It describes a trait that everyone has to a greater or lesser degree.

Do you recognise yourself strongly in very intense reactions, and also in other things such as restlessness, trouble concentrating or impulsiveness? Then it is worth discussing this with your doctor or a psychologist. A test on a website cannot assess that for you.

## What helps when criticism hits hard

You cannot simply switch off a sensitive alarm system. You can learn to respond to it differently. These steps help most people.

**1. Name what is happening.** Say to yourself: "This is my rejection sensitivity." It sounds simple, but it creates a little distance. You are not the alarm. You are the person hearing it.

**2. Wait before you respond.** The first wave of emotion is usually the strongest and passes within an hour or so. Do not send that defensive email or that message asking whether everything is all right during that wave. If possible, sleep on it.

**3. Separate what was said from what you heard.** Take two columns. Left: the literal words. Right: what you made of them. "Your report could be shorter" on the left. "I am bad at my job" on the right. Seeing it on paper often makes the gap visible.

**4. Ask a clarifying question.** Instead of guessing, ask: "Which part did you find too long?" or "Did you mean that as a joke or are you really annoyed?" Most people answer more kindly than your fear predicted.

**5. Look for the useful part.** Criticism is not always fair. But often there is a useful piece in it. You can take that piece and leave the rest. "The reports could be more concise" can simply be a tip, without saying anything about your worth.

**6. Reduce avoidance in small steps.** Every time you do not ask something out of fear of a no, the fear grows. Start small. Ask a colleague for help, suggest a plan to a friend, share an idea in a meeting. Then notice what actually happens.

**7. Talk to yourself the way you would talk to a friend.** If a friend told you about that one remark in the review, you would not say: "So you are a failure." You would put it into perspective. You deserve the same voice.

**8. Tell the people close to you.** A partner or good friend who knows that a short "ok" can unsettle you will more easily add a word. That is not weakness, it is good communication.

Many people who are sensitive to rejection also tend to overthink. If you lie awake replaying a conversation, our article on [the signs that you are an overthinker](/en/blog/signs-you-are-an-overthinker) has concrete tips for those evenings.

## What the rejection sensitivity test does and does not tell you

Our [rejection sensitivity test](/en/rejection-sensitivity) consists of statements about everyday situations. You indicate how much you agree with each one. Afterwards you see a score on the four sides from this article: anticipating rejection, perceiving rejection, reacting strongly and avoiding. For each side you get an explanation and practical tips.

That helps because "I am sensitive" is vague. Someone who mainly avoids needs different steps than someone who mainly reacts strongly. Knowing your strongest side tells you where to start.

What the test does not do: it does not give a diagnosis, it does not say anything about ADHD or any other condition, and it does not know your history. Your score is also a snapshot. After a difficult period, such as a breakup or a reorganisation at work, it can be higher than usual.

Rejection sensitivity is human. But if the fear of rejection keeps you from applying for jobs, making friends or saying what you think, it can help to talk to a psychologist. Approaches such as cognitive behavioural therapy work well for many people with this pattern.

The next time a small comment hits hard, give yourself an hour before you draw a conclusion. And if you want to know which side of rejection sensitivity is strongest for you, the [free test](/en/rejection-sensitivity) takes about five minutes.
