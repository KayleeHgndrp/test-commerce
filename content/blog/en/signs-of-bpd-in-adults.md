Borderline personality disorder (BPD) shows up as a long lasting pattern of very intense emotions that change quickly, a strong fear of being abandoned, relationships that swing between closeness and conflict, and an unstable sense of who you are. Everyone recognises a few of these feelings now and then. With BPD, they are present for years, in many areas of life, and they cause real problems.

Maybe you are reading this because a friend once said "that sounds like borderline", or because you recognise yourself in videos about BPD. Below you will find the nine signs in plain language, three situations from everyday life, how BPD differs from conditions that look like it, and what a self-test can and cannot tell you.

## What is borderline personality disorder?

BPD is a personality disorder. That sounds heavy, but it simply means that certain ways of feeling, thinking and reacting are so deeply rooted and so strong that they keep getting in your way. It is not a character flaw and not a sign of weakness.

The two handbooks clinicians use describe it slightly differently. The American DSM-5 speaks of borderline personality disorder and lists nine criteria, of which at least five must be present. The ICD-11 of the World Health Organization uses a "borderline pattern" within personality disorder. In the UK you will also hear the name emotionally unstable personality disorder (EUPD). They all describe roughly the same picture.

The signs usually become visible in the late teens or early twenties. Researchers see a mix of causes: a sensitive temperament, experiences in childhood such as an unsafe or invalidating environment, and sometimes trauma. Not everyone with BPD has experienced trauma, and not everyone who has experienced trauma develops BPD.

## The nine signs of BPD in plain language

These are the nine criteria from the DSM-5, translated into what they feel like from the inside.

1. **Fear of being left.** You do a lot to prevent someone from leaving you, even when there is no real sign that they will. A cancelled plan can feel like the start of the end.
2. **Relationships that swing.** You can see someone as wonderful one week and as a disappointment the next. Psychologists call this switching between idealising and devaluing.
3. **An unstable sense of self.** Your opinions, goals, style or even values change a lot, depending on who you are with. Many people describe it as "I don't really know who I am".
4. **Impulsiveness that hurts you.** At least two areas in which you act on impulse in a way that can harm you: spending, eating, drinking, drugs, reckless driving or sex.
5. **Self harm or suicidal thoughts.** Recurring thoughts about suicide, threats or self harm, often at moments when the emotions become unbearable.
6. **Emotions that change fast.** Intense sadness, irritation or anxiety that usually lasts a few hours and rarely more than a few days.
7. **A chronic feeling of emptiness.** Not sadness exactly, more a hollow, flat feeling that you try to fill.
8. **Intense anger that is hard to control.** Angry outbursts, bitterness or sarcasm that you regret afterwards, followed by shame.
9. **Feeling unreal or suspicious under stress.** When stress runs high, you can feel as if you are outside yourself (dissociation) or suddenly distrust people. This usually passes when the stress goes down.

The word that matters most is pattern. One evening of anger after a bad day is human. A diagnosis only fits when at least five of these signs have been present for a long time and in different situations.

## What BPD looks like in everyday life

Lists are abstract. These three situations show how the signs work together.

**The message that doesn't come.** Sara's boyfriend said he would call after football practice. It is 22:15 and her phone stays quiet. Within twenty minutes, Sara has gone from "he's probably talking to his friends" to "he's done with me". She sends four messages, the last one angry. When he calls at 22:40 and says his battery was dead, the relief is enormous, but so is the shame about those messages. The next day she feels empty and wonders why she always "ruins everything".

What you see here is the fear of abandonment, emotions that rise very fast and the anger that turns into shame. If you recognise the waiting and the panic but not the rest, our article about [anxious attachment and texting](/en/blog/anxious-attachment-texting) may fit you better.

**The manager who goes from hero to villain.** In his first month in a new job, Daan thinks his manager is the best boss he has ever had. She takes him seriously and he works evenings to impress her. Then she gives him feedback on a report in a team meeting. Daan feels humiliated and is convinced she never liked him. At home he writes a resignation letter, deletes it, writes it again. A week later he hardly remembers why he was so angry, but the relationship has cooled.

This is the swing between idealising and devaluing, combined with a strong reaction to criticism. That reaction alone is not BPD. Many people are simply very sensitive to rejection, which we explain in [why small criticism hits so hard](/en/blog/rejection-sensitivity-criticism).

**The Sunday that needs filling.** Lina has a full week of work, friends and plans. On Sunday afternoon, when everything goes quiet, a gnawing emptiness arrives. She orders clothes she doesn't need, drinks half a bottle of wine and scrolls dating apps until 2 a.m. She has changed study three times and her friends tease her that she has a new "personality" with every new group of friends. She laughs about it, but deep down it scares her.

Here you see chronic emptiness, impulsiveness and an unstable sense of self. None of these alone proves anything. Together, and for years, they fit the picture.

## Quiet BPD: when it is all on the inside

Not everyone with borderline traits slams doors. Some people turn everything inward. They swallow their anger, withdraw when they feel rejected, blame themselves for everything and feel ashamed for days. From the outside they seem calm and capable, sometimes even the person everyone leans on.

"Quiet BPD" is not an official diagnosis, but the term helps many people recognise themselves. Because the struggle is invisible, it is often noticed late, sometimes only after years of burnout, depression or eating problems.

## BPD or something else? Conditions that look alike

Many signs of BPD also appear in other conditions. That is one reason why a self diagnosis is risky.

- **Bipolar disorder.** Also intense moods, but in episodes of days or weeks, with clear changes in sleep and energy. In BPD, emotions often change within hours and in response to people. Read more in [bipolar or mood swings](/en/blog/bipolar-or-mood-swings).
- **Rejection sensitivity and anxious attachment.** A strong fear of rejection and a lot of overthinking after a late reply, but without the impulsiveness, emptiness and identity problems. The [four attachment styles](/en/blog/attachment-styles-explained) explain where that fear can come from.
- **Complex trauma (C-PTSD).** Overlaps a lot with BPD: emotional dysregulation, a negative self image and difficulties in relationships. The difference lies mostly in how stable the self image is and how relationships are experienced.
- **ADHD.** Impulsiveness and fast changing moods are also part of ADHD, especially in adults. The cause and the treatment are different.
- **Depression and anxiety disorders.** These often occur together with BPD and can colour the picture.

Conditions can also occur together. A professional takes the time to sort that out.

## How do I know if I have BPD?

This is the question many people type into a search bar. These questions help you look honestly at yourself, without jumping to conclusions.

- **How long has this been going on?** BPD is visible over years, often since adolescence. A difficult period after a breakup or a death is not the same.
- **Does it happen in many places?** With a partner, but also with friends, at work and with family? Or only in one relationship that is genuinely unsafe?
- **How fast does your mood switch?** Within hours, and often because of something someone says or does?
- **What happens after the storm?** Many people with BPD describe intense shame, emptiness or self hatred after an outburst.
- **What do others say?** Do people close to you mention the same patterns, such as "you see me as either perfect or terrible"?
- **What does it cost you?** Lost jobs, friendships that keep ending, debts, injuries or exhaustion?

If you answer yes to many of these and it has been that way for a long time, it is worth talking to a professional. Not to put a label on yourself, but because there is help that works.

## What a BPD self-test can and cannot tell you

Self-tests for BPD are based on questionnaires that researchers developed to screen for borderline traits. A well known example is the McLean Screening Instrument (MSI-BPD) by Mary Zanarini and colleagues from 2003: ten yes or no questions that follow the DSM criteria. Questionnaires like this are used to decide who might benefit from a more thorough assessment.

Our [free BPD test](/en/bpd) works in the same way. You answer statements about how you usually feel and react, and you see which borderline traits are strong for you and which are not.

What a test can do:

- Give you words for something you feel but find hard to describe.
- Show whether your answers resemble patterns that fit BPD.
- Help you decide whether to talk to your GP or a psychologist, and give you something concrete to bring up.

What a test cannot do:

- Make a diagnosis. That requires a conversation about your life story, often with structured interviews, and a check for other explanations.
- Take your current state into account. If you take the test on a terrible day, you will score higher than on a calm day.
- Tell the difference between BPD, complex trauma, ADHD or bipolar disorder. Their symptoms overlap too much for that.

Take the result as a starting point for a conversation, not as an answer.

## What you can do now

Whether or not you have BPD, these steps help when emotions overwhelm you.

**1. Keep track for two weeks.** Write down every evening what you felt, how strongly (0 to 10), what triggered it and what you did. After two weeks you see patterns, and you have something concrete to discuss with a professional.

**2. Build in a pause.** Intense emotions peak and then fall, often within half an hour. Agree with yourself that you won't send messages or make big decisions at a 9 or 10. Save the message as a draft and read it again after an hour.

**3. Calm your body first.** Cold water on your face, a short fast walk or slow breathing with a longer exhale lower the intensity. These are techniques from dialectical behaviour therapy (DBT), the therapy Marsha Linehan developed specifically for BPD.

**4. Name what you feel.** "I am scared he'll leave" is more manageable than a cloud of panic. Naming an emotion helps you see that it is a feeling and not a fact.

**5. Tell one person.** Shame thrives on secrecy. One person who knows what you are dealing with can help you check your thoughts when the fear takes over.

**6. Ask for help.** Start with your GP and say plainly what you notice. Effective treatments exist, such as DBT, mentalization based treatment (MBT) by Bateman and Fonagy and schema therapy. Long term research from McLean Hospital shows that symptoms decrease over time for most people.

If you have thoughts of harming yourself or ending your life, don't wait. Contact your local emergency number or a crisis line right now. You don't have to go through this alone.

## If someone close to you has BPD

Living with someone with BPD can be intense. A few things help: stay calm and predictable, take the emotion seriously without agreeing with every interpretation ("I can see you're scared, and I'm not leaving"), set clear limits and look after yourself. Encourage professional help without pushing.

Borderline is a heavy word, but it describes something many people live with and get better from. If you want to know where you stand, take the [BPD test](/en/bpd) and use the result to start an honest conversation with yourself or with someone who can help.
