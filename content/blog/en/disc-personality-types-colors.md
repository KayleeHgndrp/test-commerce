A DISC profile describes how you tend to behave, especially at work, along four styles: **D for Dominance (red)**, **I for Influence (yellow)**, **S for Steadiness (green)** and **C for Conscientiousness (blue)**. Almost everyone uses all four, just not equally. Your profile shows which style comes most naturally to you and which one costs you the most energy.

That is useful because most friction at work is not about bad intentions. It is about four people who want different things from the same meeting. Below you will find what each colour means, three everyday situations where you can see them clash, how to recognise your own style and what a DISC test can and cannot tell you.

## What is a DISC profile?

The model goes back to the American psychologist William Moulton Marston. In his 1928 book *Emotions of Normal People* he described four ways in which people respond to their surroundings. Marston himself never built a test. Later psychologists turned his ideas into questionnaires: Walter Clarke in the 1950s and John Geier in the 1970s. Most DISC tests you see today, including the free ones, descend from that work.

The colours came even later. They are a memory aid, and many trainers and books use them. In Scandinavia the Swedish author Thomas Erikson made them famous with his book *Surrounded by Idiots* (2014). That is why many people now say "he is very red" instead of "he scores high on D".

The simplest way to understand DISC is through two questions:

- **Fast or calm?** Do you tend to act, speak up and push forward (D and I), or do you prefer to observe, think and take your time (S and C)?
- **Tasks or people?** Is your attention mainly on the job and the result (D and C), or on the people and the atmosphere (I and S)?

Combine the two and you get four styles. D is fast and task focused. I is fast and people focused. S is calm and people focused. C is calm and task focused. None of them is better. Each one is strong in some situations and awkward in others.

## The four DISC types: red, yellow, green and blue

### Red: Dominance (D)

Red people want results, and preferably soon. They make decisions quickly, say what they think and do not mind a bit of conflict if it moves things forward. In a crisis they are the ones who say "Right, this is what we are going to do".

Their pitfall is speed without patience. They can come across as blunt, cut people off and decide before everyone has spoken. Under pressure they get more controlling. An email from a red colleague is often three lines long and ends with a deadline.

### Yellow: Influence (I)

Yellow people bring energy. They like ideas, people and new plans, and they are good at getting others excited. They think out loud, tell stories and make a team feel like a team.

Their pitfall is follow through. They promise a lot, get distracted by the next idea and find details tedious. Under pressure they talk more and plan less. A yellow message might contain three exclamation marks, a joke and a question that was not really the point.

### Green: Steadiness (S)

Green people are calm, loyal and patient. They listen well, keep the peace and make sure nobody is left behind. Teams with a strong green member are often teams where people feel safe.

Their pitfall is that they keep quiet too long. They dislike sudden change and avoid conflict, so they say yes when they mean no and swallow their irritation until it finally comes out. If that sounds familiar, our article on [how to say no without feeling guilty](/en/blog/how-to-say-no-without-guilt) is worth reading. Under pressure, green people withdraw and go along with things they do not agree with.

### Blue: Conscientiousness (C)

Blue people want to get it right. They are precise, critical in a useful way and like a plan with facts behind it. They notice the error on page 14 that everyone else missed.

Their pitfall is that "right" becomes "perfect". They can hesitate for a long time, ask for one more analysis and come across as cool or distant. Under pressure they dig into details and become more critical. A blue email often has numbered points and a question about the source.

## Three everyday situations where you see the four styles

### The Monday meeting with a new plan

Your manager announces that the team will switch to a new planning tool next month. Watch what happens around the table.

Red Maria asks straight away when it starts and who is in charge. Yellow Tom thinks it is a great idea and is already suggesting a name for the team channel. Green Sanne says little, but you can see her thinking about everything that will change in her routine. Blue Daniel asks why this tool, what it costs and whether anyone has compared it with the alternatives.

Nobody is being difficult. Maria needs clarity, Tom needs enthusiasm, Sanne needs time and Daniel needs reasons. A manager who knows this gives all four: a clear decision, a bit of energy, a realistic timeline and the background in writing.

### The group chat planning a birthday weekend

Six friends want to book a cottage for a birthday. Yellow sends twelve links and a voice message within an hour. Red says "Let's take the second one, I'll book it tonight unless someone objects". Blue asks about cancellation policy and whether there is a dishwasher. Green replies "fine with me" to everything, even though the cottage is a three hour drive and they have to work on Friday.

You probably recognise at least one of them. The risk here is with green. Because they do not object, nobody notices they are unhappy until they cancel at the last minute. A simple "Does this really work for everyone?" from the group would have helped.

### Feedback on a report

You hand in a report and get feedback from a red manager: "Too long. Cut it in half and put the conclusion first." For a red person this is simply efficient. For a blue colleague it feels like all the careful work was ignored. For a green colleague the short tone may sound like disapproval, even when it was not meant that way.

If short, direct feedback hits you harder than you would like, our article on [why small criticism hurts so much](/en/blog/rejection-sensitivity-criticism) explains what is going on. And if you are the red manager, one extra sentence often makes a big difference: "The content is good, the format needs to be shorter."

## Which DISC type am I? How to recognise yours

You can get a long way with a few honest questions. Answer them from how you usually behave, not from how you would like to be.

- **What drains you most?** Slow decisions (red), working alone all day without contact (yellow), constant change and conflict (green), or sloppy work and vague plans (blue)?
- **What do you do under pressure?** Take control, talk more, go quiet, or bury yourself in the details?
- **What feedback do you hear more than once?** "You can be a bit abrupt" points to red. "You start a lot and finish little" points to yellow. "We never know what you really think" points to green. "You are so critical" points to blue.
- **What do your messages look like?** Short and to the point, full of emojis, polite and careful, or structured and complete?

Most people recognise themselves in two colours. That is normal. Common combinations are red with blue (fast, demanding and precise), yellow with green (warm, social and helpful) and red with yellow (energetic and persuasive). Your second colour often explains why the description of your main colour does not fully fit.

Also watch for the difference between work and home. Plenty of people are blue at the office and yellow with friends. If you take a test, think about one setting while you answer.

## How to work with each colour

Knowing your own colour is useful. Knowing what someone else needs is even more useful. A few practical guidelines:

**With red:** get to the point. Start with the conclusion, give two options and a recommendation, and do not take a short reply personally. If you disagree, say so clearly. Red respects people who push back with arguments.

**With yellow:** leave room for chat and ideas, then put the agreements in writing. Ask "What is the first step, and when?" A short summary after the meeting saves a lot of confusion.

**With green:** give them time. Announce changes early, explain what stays the same and ask for their opinion one to one, not only in the group. Silence does not mean agreement.

**With blue:** send information in advance, be precise and admit what you do not know. Give a deadline for the analysis, otherwise it can continue for ever.

And for yourself, a few steps that help every colour:

1. **Pick your stretch.** Choose one behaviour from the opposite style to practise. Red practises asking one more question before deciding. Green practises saying "I disagree" once a week. Yellow practises finishing one thing before starting the next. Blue practises sending something at 90 percent.
2. **Name your style out loud.** "I tend to decide fast, so stop me if I am rushing" makes it easier for others to respond.
3. **Translate, do not change.** You do not have to become someone else. You only have to adjust how you deliver your message to the person in front of you.
4. **Watch your pressure pattern.** The pitfall of each colour gets bigger under stress. Blue people in particular may recognise themselves in our article on [the signs of overthinking](/en/blog/signs-you-are-an-overthinker).

## What a DISC test says and does not say

DISC is a handy language for talking about differences. It gives teams words for things that otherwise turn into irritation. That is its value. It is good to be honest about its limits too.

**It measures behaviour, not ability.** A high C does not mean you are smarter, and a low D does not mean you cannot lead. Good leaders exist in every colour.

**It is based on self-report.** You answer questions about yourself, so the result shows how you see yourself on that day and in that setting. Ask a colleague how they see you and the picture may differ.

**It has less scientific support than the Big Five.** Most personality research uses the Big Five model, which measures five broad traits. DISC is mainly a practical tool from the world of training and coaching. Many commercial versions have not been independently tested. If you want a broader and better researched picture of your personality, the [Big Five](/en/blog/big-five-explained) is the stronger choice. You can also take our [Big Five test](/en/big-five).

**It is not a box.** "I am just red" is not an excuse to steamroll people, and "she is blue, she is always difficult" is not a fair description of a colleague. The colours describe a preference, not a fixed identity.

**It should never decide who gets a job.** A DISC profile can start a good conversation in a team. It is not suitable for selecting or rejecting people.

Used this way, a DISC test is simply a mirror with four colours. It shows a pattern, and you decide what to do with it.

## Why this matters more than it seems

Many workplace conflicts look personal but are really about style. The red manager thinks the blue colleague is slow. The blue colleague thinks the red manager is careless. Both are partly right, and both need each other. Teams with only one colour tend to have a blind spot: all red teams move fast in the wrong direction, all green teams avoid the conversation that needs to happen.

Once you see the four styles, you start reading meetings differently. You notice who has not spoken yet, who needs the numbers and who needs the decision. That small shift often does more for a team than a new tool or a reorganisation.
