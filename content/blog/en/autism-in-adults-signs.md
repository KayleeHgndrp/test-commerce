Autism in adults usually shows up as difficulty reading the unwritten rules of social contact, a strong need for predictability and routine, deep interests and a stronger reaction to sound, light or touch. You do not become autistic as an adult. The traits were there in childhood, but many people only recognise them much later, often because they learned to hide them or because nobody was looking.

Perhaps you are reading this because a friend or a video described your inner life a little too well, because your child is being assessed and you recognise yourself, or because you have always felt slightly out of step without knowing why. Below you will find the signs in plain language, three situations from daily life, what masking is, how to look at yourself honestly, and what a self-test can and cannot tell you.

## What is autism, exactly?

Autism is a neurodevelopmental condition. That means it has to do with how the brain develops and processes information, not with upbringing, coldness or a lack of effort. It is lifelong, and it shows up very differently from person to person. That is why clinicians speak of the autism spectrum.

The DSM-5, the handbook many clinicians use, describes two areas. The first is persistent differences in social communication and interaction: the back and forth of conversation, nonverbal signals such as facial expressions and tone of voice, and building and keeping relationships. The second is restricted or repetitive patterns: routines, intense interests, repeated movements, and being over or under sensitive to sensory input. The traits must have been present from early childhood, even if they only became a problem later when life got more demanding.

Since 2013, Asperger syndrome is no longer a separate diagnosis. It falls under autism spectrum disorder. Many adults still use the word, and many people now prefer to say "autistic person" rather than "person with autism". Both are fine. What matters is how you want to describe yourself.

## The signs of autism in adults, in plain language

Most descriptions of autism were written with young boys in mind. Here is what the same traits often look like in an adult life. You do not need to recognise all of them, and recognising some of them does not mean you are autistic.

**Social contact and communication**

- **Small talk feels like a test.** Chatting about the weather or the weekend feels pointless or tiring, while a deep conversation about something that interests you can go on for hours.
- **Unwritten rules stay unwritten.** You are not always sure when it is your turn to speak, how long to keep eye contact, or what someone really means when they say "we should get coffee sometime".
- **You take things literally.** Sarcasm, hints and vague requests can pass you by, or you need a moment longer to work them out.
- **You are told you come across as blunt.** You say what you think because it is true, and only later notice that someone was hurt.
- **A social day needs recovery time.** After a party, a busy workday or a family visit, you need hours or even a day alone to recharge.

**Routines and interests**

- **Change is hard, especially at short notice.** A cancelled plan, a new route to work or a changed meeting time can throw off your whole day, even when the change is small.
- **Deep interests.** You can become absorbed in a subject and know everything about it, from trains to a TV series to a scientific field. In women these interests are often less unusual on the surface, such as psychology, animals or books, so they are less likely to be noticed.
- **Fixed ways of doing things.** The same breakfast, the same seat, the same order when you get ready. It gives you rest.

**Senses**

- **Sound, light and smells come in harder.** The hum of a fridge, fluorescent light in an office, perfume on public transport or a scratchy label in a shirt can feel unbearable.
- **Crowded places drain you fast.** A supermarket on Saturday or a busy restaurant can leave you exhausted or irritable.
- **Sometimes the opposite.** Some autistic people notice pain, hunger or temperature less, or seek out strong sensations.

Many autistic adults also describe moments of overload. When there is too much at once, you can shut down and go quiet, or you can snap, cry or need to leave immediately. These are sometimes called shutdowns and meltdowns. They are not tantrums, but a nervous system that has reached its limit.

## Three situations from everyday life

Lists are abstract. These three examples show how the traits can work together in an ordinary life.

**The office party.** Daniel is 34 and a software developer. His team appreciates him because he spots mistakes nobody else sees. He dreads the annual office party for weeks. He prepares topics to talk about, practises a few jokes and plans what time he can leave without looking rude. During the evening he stands near the door, nods in the right places and smiles when others laugh. At home he lies on the sofa in the dark for an hour, and he needs the whole next day to feel normal again. His colleagues think he had a nice time.

What you see here is not shyness in the usual sense. Daniel likes his colleagues. The effort is in the constant translating: what does this joke mean, is it my turn now, how long have I been looking at this person.

**The friend who seems to have it all together.** Sara is 41, a nurse, and the person everyone calls when they need help. As a child she was quiet and studied other girls closely to see how they talked and dressed. At secondary school she copied a popular classmate's way of laughing. Now she has a rich social life on paper, but she has been treated twice for burnout and once for anxiety. When her daughter is referred for an autism assessment and Sara fills in the questionnaire about her daughter's childhood, she realises she is also describing her own.

This is a story many women recognise. Their traits were hidden behind good grades, care for others and enormous effort. The price often only becomes visible years later.

**The changed plan.** Thomas has planned his Saturday precisely: groceries at nine when it is quiet, then the gym, then cooking. His partner suggests at breakfast that they visit her parents instead. It is a kind idea, and Thomas likes her parents. Yet he feels panic rising, says no more sharply than he means, and spends the rest of the morning in a bad mood. Later he can explain it: it was not the visit, it was the sudden loss of a day he had already lived in his head.

## What is masking, and why does it hide autism?

Masking, or camouflaging, means hiding your autistic traits or compensating for them to fit in. Researchers have studied it in recent years, for example with the Camouflaging Autistic Traits Questionnaire (CAT-Q), developed by Laura Hull and colleagues. Examples include:

- forcing yourself to make eye contact, or looking at someone's nose or eyebrows instead;
- copying the facial expressions, phrases and laugh of people around you;
- preparing conversations in advance and replaying them afterwards;
- holding back movements that calm you, such as rocking or fidgeting with your hands;
- going along with plans you do not want, so that you do not stand out.

The NHS notes that autistic women in particular may copy others and appear to cope well socially, which makes the signs harder to see. Masking works, in the sense that others notice less. But it takes a lot of energy, and it is linked to exhaustion, anxiety and depression. Many adults are first treated for those problems, and the autism underneath stays out of sight.

Masking also often goes together with trying hard to keep others happy. And if a short comment can occupy you for days, read about [why small criticism hits so hard](/en/blog/rejection-sensitivity-criticism).

## Could I be autistic? How to look at yourself honestly

Everyone dislikes some noise, everyone finds certain parties tiring and everyone has habits. The difference lies in how strong, how constant and how costly it is. These questions can help you sort it out:

1. **Was it already there in childhood?** Think of how you played, whether you had one close friend or none, whether you had intense interests or strong reactions to clothes, food or noise. If you can, ask your parents or look at old school reports.
2. **Does it show up everywhere?** At work, at home, with friends and with family, not only during one stressful period.
3. **What does it cost you?** Frequent exhaustion after social contact, burnout, conflicts you do not understand, or the feeling that you are always performing.
4. **Is there another explanation?** Social anxiety, ADHD, trauma, depression or high sensitivity can look similar in some ways. They can also occur together with autism.

That last point is important. ADHD and autism, for example, often occur together. If you also recognise yourself in chaos, forgetfulness and inner restlessness, our article on [ADHD in adults](/en/blog/adhd-in-adults-signs) explains the differences and the overlap. And high sensitivity can look like sensory sensitivity, but without the social and routine part. The [HSP test](/en/hsp) can help you see which fits better.

## What an online autism test does and does not tell you

Screeners for adults have existed for a long time. The best known are the Autism Spectrum Quotient (AQ), developed by Simon Baron-Cohen and colleagues at the University of Cambridge, its short version the AQ-10, which the British NICE guideline on autism in adults mentions as a first step, and the RAADS-R. They ask about the right things, but they are meant as a filter, not as a diagnosis.

Our [free autism test](/en/autism) works in a similar way. You respond to statements about how you usually are, and you see how strongly you recognise traits in four areas: social interaction, communication, sensory sensitivity, and routines or focused interests.

**What the test can do:**

- Show which areas stand out for you and which do not.
- Give you words for experiences you have had for years.
- Help you decide whether an assessment is worth it, and give you concrete examples to bring to your doctor.

**What the test cannot do:**

- Make a diagnosis. Only a psychologist or psychiatrist with experience in adult autism can do that, usually after detailed interviews and often with input from someone who knew you as a child.
- Rule out other explanations. Social anxiety or high sensitivity can give similar answers on some questions.
- See through masking. If you have spent years adapting, you may answer based on how you behave rather than on how it feels inside. Some people score lower than their daily reality would suggest. A low score is not proof that you are not autistic.

## What you can do now

Whether or not you are autistic, these steps often help people who recognise themselves in the signs.

1. **Write down examples.** Not "I find social things hard", but "after a birthday party I need a whole day in bed" or "I left a job because of the open plan office". Concrete examples help a professional much more than general statements.
2. **Talk to your GP.** In most countries the GP is the first step and can refer you to a team that assesses adults. Waiting lists can be long, so it helps to start early.
3. **Plan recovery time.** If you know a busy day is coming, keep the evening or the next morning free. That is not weakness, it is maintenance.
4. **Reduce what you can.** Noise cancelling headphones, sunglasses in bright shops, doing groceries at quiet times, asking for an agenda before meetings. Small changes can save a lot of energy.
5. **Read and listen to autistic adults.** Many people find more recognition in the stories of others than in any list. Just remember that recognising yourself is a reason to look further, not a diagnosis.

If you are diagnosed, there is no medication for autism itself. Support usually consists of information, practical adjustments at work or at home, and help with things that often come along, such as anxiety, sleep problems or burnout. Many adults say that the explanation alone already changed how they look at their past.

## When to look for help sooner

Sometimes the problems are heavier than a tiring party. Look for help soon if you have been exhausted for weeks and cannot recover, if you are at risk of losing your job or study place, if you stop eating or sleeping properly, or if you feel down or hopeless. If you have thoughts of ending your life, contact your GP, an emergency number or a crisis line in your country today. You do not need to know whether it is autism before you ask for help.

Recognising yourself in the signs of autism can bring relief, doubt and a lot of questions at the same time. Start with what you notice in your own life. If you want a clearer picture first, take the [autism test](/en/autism) and bring your results to a professional who can look at the whole story.
