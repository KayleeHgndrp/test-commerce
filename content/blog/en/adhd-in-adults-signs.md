ADHD in adults usually shows up as trouble keeping your attention on things that do not grab you, an inner restlessness that makes it hard to relax, and acting or speaking before you have thought it through. It is not something you develop as an adult: the signs were there in childhood, but many people only recognise them years later, especially women and people who learned to compensate well.

Maybe you are reading this because you keep losing your keys, because a friend with ADHD described your life a little too accurately, or because you are exhausted from working twice as hard as everyone else to keep up. Below you will find the signs in plain language, three situations from daily life, how ADHD can look different in women, and what a self-test can and cannot tell you.

## What is ADHD, exactly?

ADHD stands for attention deficit hyperactivity disorder. It is a neurodevelopmental condition, which means it has to do with how the brain develops and works, not with laziness, upbringing or a lack of willpower. It runs strongly in families.

Clinicians usually work with the DSM-5, the American handbook of mental disorders. It describes two groups of signs: inattention on the one hand, hyperactivity and impulsivity on the other. For adults, at least five signs from one or both groups must be present for at least six months. Several of them must have been there before the age of twelve, they must show up in more than one setting, for example at work and at home, and they must really get in the way.

Depending on which group is strongest, there are three presentations: mainly inattentive, mainly hyperactive and impulsive, or combined. The mainly inattentive presentation is what many people still call ADD.

## The signs of ADHD in adults, in plain language

The official lists were written with children in mind. Here is what the same signs often look like in an adult life.

**Attention**

- **Drifting off.** You read a page and realise you took nothing in. In meetings or long conversations your thoughts go their own way, even when you want to listen.
- **Starting is the hardest part.** Boring tasks, like filling in a form or answering an email, can stay on your list for weeks, even when they would take ten minutes.
- **Losing things and forgetting.** Keys, phone, the reason you walked into the kitchen. Appointments slip through, bills get paid late, not because you do not care.
- **Careless mistakes.** You overlook details in emails or documents, especially in work that bores you.
- **Hyperfocus.** The other side of the coin. When something really interests you, you can work on it for hours and forget to eat. Hyperfocus is not an official criterion, but many adults with ADHD recognise it.

**Restlessness and impulsivity**

- **Inner restlessness.** As a child you may have climbed on furniture. As an adult it is more often a motor inside: a busy head, a bouncing leg, trouble sitting through a long film or a slow dinner.
- **Speaking before thinking.** You finish other people's sentences, interrupt, or say something you regret a minute later.
- **Impatience.** Waiting in a queue or for a slow website feels physically uncomfortable.
- **Impulsive choices.** Buying things on a whim, changing plans suddenly, quitting something abruptly when your patience runs out.

**Time and planning**

Many adults struggle most with what psychologists call executive functions: planning, prioritising and keeping track of time. You underestimate how long things take, you are late even when you left on time, and a big project feels like a wall without a door. People often describe this as time blindness. It is not an official term, but it captures the feeling well.

Strong emotions also come up a lot. Many adults with ADHD say they get irritated quickly, feel rejection deeply and have trouble calming down. That is not part of the official criteria, but it is often reported and it can make daily life heavier.

## Three situations from everyday life

Lists are abstract. These three examples show how the signs work together.

**The tax return that waits until the deadline.** Mark is good at his job as a sound technician. Under pressure on a live show, he is calm and fast. But his tax return has been on his desk for six weeks. Every evening he plans to do it and every evening something else happens: he cleans the fridge, reads about synthesizers, scrolls until midnight. Two days before the deadline he finally does it in one feverish evening, with a fine for a missing document. Afterwards he feels stupid, because "a child could do this".

What you see here is the trouble starting dull tasks, the pull towards interesting things and the help of a deadline that finally provides enough pressure. Procrastination on its own is very human, and not everyone who procrastinates has ADHD. If this pattern is mainly about fear of failing, our [procrastination test](/en/procrastination) may tell you more.

**The mother who seems to have everything under control.** Lisa is 38, has two children and a part time job, and everyone sees her as organised. What nobody sees is how she does it: three alarms for every appointment, lists on every wall, and evenings spent catching up on things she forgot during the day. In meetings she takes notes on everything, because otherwise the words disappear. When her son is assessed for ADHD and the psychologist asks questions about his childhood, Lisa recognises herself in almost every answer.

This is a common story. Many women only start to wonder about ADHD when their child is assessed. Their systems kept them going for years, but at a high price.

**The colleague who interrupts.** Ahmed has a hundred ideas in every brainstorm and colleagues love his energy. But he also interrupts them, sends emails he should have read twice, and changes direction halfway through projects. In his annual review his manager praises his creativity and asks him, again, to "finish things first". Ahmed knows that, and he tries. At home he is exhausted from the effort of holding himself back all day.

## Why ADHD in women is often recognised late

For a long time, the image of ADHD was a restless boy who could not sit still in class. Research and diagnostic criteria were largely based on boys. Girls who were dreamy, chatty or chaotic but not disruptive were less likely to be noticed.

Several things play a role:

- **The inattentive form is more common in girls and women.** Dreaminess and forgetfulness bother a teacher less than a child who runs around the classroom.
- **Masking and compensating.** Many women build strategies to hide their struggles: perfectionism, extreme preparation, working late. It works, until the load becomes too great, for example after having children or in a demanding job.
- **Other labels come first.** Women with ADHD are often first seen for anxiety, depression, burnout or sleep problems. Those problems can be real, and sometimes the ADHD underneath remains out of sight.

Common signs in women include a constantly busy head, feeling overwhelmed by household tasks and paperwork, being called scatterbrained or chaotic as a child, and a strong sensitivity to criticism. ## How do I know if I have ADHD?

Everyone loses their keys sometimes and everyone drifts off in a boring meeting. The difference lies in three things: how often it happens, how long it has been going on, and how much it costs you.

Ask yourself these questions:

1. **Was it already there in childhood?** Think of school reports with remarks like "could do better if she concentrated" or "talks a lot in class". If you can, ask your parents or look at old reports.
2. **Does it show up everywhere?** At work, at home, with friends and in your finances, not only in one stressful job.
3. **Does it really get in the way?** Missed deadlines, conflicts, debts, chronic exhaustion from compensating.
4. **Is there another explanation?** Poor sleep, a period of heavy stress, anxiety, depression or a thyroid problem can all cause concentration problems. A good assessment looks at these too.

ADHD is also often combined with other things. Anxiety, low mood, sleep problems and autism traits occur more often in people with ADHD than in the general population. That does not make things simpler, but it is a reason to take an assessment seriously and not draw conclusions too quickly yourself.

## What an online ADHD test does and does not tell you

The best known screener for adults is the Adult ADHD Self Report Scale (ASRS), developed with the World Health Organization. Many clinics use a version of it as a first step. Screeners like this are useful because they ask about the right things. But they are not a diagnosis.

Our [free ADHD test](/en/adhd) works in a similar way. You answer statements about how often something has applied to you in the last six months, and you see how strongly you recognise traits in four areas: inattention, hyperactivity, impulsivity and executive functions such as planning and time.

**What the test can do:**

- Show which areas stand out for you and which do not.
- Give you words for things you have experienced for years.
- Help you decide whether an assessment is worth it, and give you concrete examples to bring to your doctor.

**What the test cannot do:**

- Make a diagnosis. Only a psychiatrist or psychologist can do that, usually after a detailed interview about your childhood and current life.
- Rule out other causes. Anxiety or poor sleep can give similar answers.
- See what you have learned to hide. Some people, especially women who compensate a lot, score lower than their daily reality would suggest. A low score does not prove that you do not have ADHD.

## What you can do now

Whether or not you have ADHD, these steps often help people who recognise themselves in the signs.

1. **Write down examples.** Not "I am chaotic", but "I paid my rent late three times this year" or "I missed my sister's birthday dinner". Concrete examples help a doctor much more than general statements.
2. **Talk to your GP.** In most countries the GP is the first step and can refer you to a psychiatrist or psychologist who specialises in adult ADHD. Waiting lists can be long, so it helps to start early.
3. **Make things visible.** Calendars with reminders, a fixed spot for your keys, a timer for boring tasks. Many people with ADHD work better when things are outside their head instead of in it.
4. **Make starting smaller.** Instead of "do the tax return", choose "open the website and log in". Getting started is often the hardest part, and the next step usually follows more easily.
5. **Be kind to yourself.** Many adults with ADHD have heard for years that they are lazy or careless. Knowing that your brain works differently does not solve everything, but it can make room for a different way of looking at yourself.

If you are diagnosed, there are several forms of help. Guidelines such as the British NICE guideline on ADHD mention information and coaching, cognitive behavioural therapy and medication, often in combination. What fits depends on you and on what you struggle with most.

## When to look for help sooner

Sometimes the problems are bigger than a lost set of keys. Look for help soon if you are falling behind on rent or bills, if you are at risk of losing your job, if you are drinking or using drugs to cope, or if you feel down or hopeless for weeks. You do not need to know whether it is ADHD before you ask for help. Your GP can help you work out what is going on.

Recognising yourself in the signs of ADHD can bring relief and confusion at the same time. Start with what you notice in your own life, and if you want a clearer picture first, take the [ADHD test](/en/adhd) and bring your results to a professional who can look at the whole story.
