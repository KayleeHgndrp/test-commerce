You are probably an overthinker if you often replay conversations, picture what could go wrong before it happens, or spend far longer on small decisions than they deserve. Overthinking is not the same as being thoughtful. It is thinking that goes round in circles and leaves you more tired than before, and for many people it peaks the moment the lights go out.

The good news is that it is a habit, and habits can be shifted. Below you will find the most common signs, three situations you may recognise, why the night is so hard, and what you can do about it this evening.

## What overthinking actually is

Everyone thinks things through. The difference lies in where the thinking goes. Useful thinking ends in something: a plan, a decision, a message you send, or the conclusion that there is nothing to do. Overthinking ends nowhere. You ask the same question for the tenth time and get the same unclear answer.

Psychologists usually split this into two related habits. The first is **rumination**, a term made well known by the researcher Susan Nolen-Hoeksema. Rumination looks backwards: why did I say that, why does this always happen to me. The second is **worry**, which Thomas Borkovec and colleagues studied for decades. Worry looks forwards: what if it goes wrong, what if they are angry.

In everyday life two more patterns often join in. One is getting stuck on choices, comparing options until you are too tired to pick. The other is replaying social moments: going back over a chat, a meeting or a party to check how you came across. Most overthinkers have one or two of these that are much stronger than the others.

## Signs you are an overthinker

You do not need all of these. If five or more sound very familiar, overthinking is probably taking up a real share of your energy.

1. You replay conversations hours or days later and think of what you should have said.
2. You read your own messages back to see if they might have sounded rude.
3. A short reply like "ok" keeps you busy for longer than the whole conversation did.
4. Before an appointment, a trip or a talk with your boss, you have already run the worst version in your head.
5. You find it hard to choose something small, such as a meal, a present or a reply, and you ask others for advice you then do not follow.
6. After deciding, you keep reopening the decision.
7. You lie awake while your body is tired and your head is not.
8. You think about one mistake far longer than about ten things that went well.
9. You prepare for conversations that may never happen.
10. You feel you must understand exactly why something happened before you can let it go.
11. People tell you that you think too much, or that you should "just stop worrying".
12. Thinking about a problem makes you feel more stuck, not less.

Notice that none of these signs is about intelligence or caring too much. Many overthinkers are careful, conscientious and kind. The problem is not the quality of the thoughts, but the fact that they will not stop.

## Three moments you may recognise

### The message from your manager

It is Thursday afternoon. Your manager sends: "Can we have a quick chat tomorrow morning?" No reason, no emoji. For the rest of the evening you go through every email you sent that week. You remember a deadline that slipped by a day. By dinner you are fairly sure you are in trouble, and by bedtime you have rehearsed three versions of your defence.

The next morning the chat is about holiday planning. All that thinking did not prepare you for anything. It just cost you an evening and a good part of your sleep.

### The Sunday evening spiral

Sunday, around nine. The weekend was fine, but now the week ahead appears in your head all at once: the presentation, the dentist, the friend you still owe a call. Each item pulls in another. You open your laptop "just to check something" and close it again an hour later, more tense than before.

This is future worry in its purest form. Nothing on the list can be done on a Sunday evening, which is exactly why the mind keeps circling it.

### The birthday party replay

You went to a friend's birthday and had a nice time. Driving home, you suddenly remember a joke you made that nobody laughed at. By the time you are in bed you have replayed it a dozen times, along with the moment you interrupted someone and the look on a stranger's face.

Research on the so called spotlight effect, by Thomas Gilovich and colleagues, found that people tend to overestimate how much others notice what they do. The joke that echoes in your head for days was probably forgotten by everyone else before dessert.

## Why does overthinking get worse at night?

Many people can keep their thoughts in check during the day and lose that grip after ten in the evening. There are a few clear reasons for this.

First, distraction falls away. During the day your attention is taken by work, people, traffic and screens. In a dark, quiet bedroom the mind has nothing else to hold on to.

Second, you are tired. A tired brain is worse at putting things in perspective and quicker to see threats. A problem that looks manageable at noon can feel huge at two in the morning.

Third, there is nothing you can do. You cannot call the bank, fix the report or talk to your partner while they are asleep. Worry thrives on problems you cannot act on yet, because the mind keeps trying to solve them anyway.

Finally, the bed can become linked to thinking. If you lie awake worrying night after night, your brain learns that bed is the place where the thinking happens. That is why sleep experts often advise getting up when you cannot sleep, so the link is broken.

## How to recognise your own pattern

Overthinking looks different from person to person, and the steps that help depend on which pattern is strongest for you. Ask yourself these questions over the next few days, ideally right after you catch yourself thinking in circles:

- **Which direction am I facing?** Am I going over the past (rumination) or rehearsing the future (worry)?
- **Is this about a choice?** Am I stuck between options and afraid of picking the wrong one?
- **Is this about people?** Am I replaying how I came across, what someone meant or what they think of me?
- **Is there a next step?** Is there something concrete I can do in the next 24 hours, or am I going over something I cannot change?
- **How do I feel after ten minutes?** Clearer, or more tense?

Write the answers down for a week. Most people are surprised how often the same theme comes back. If you want a more structured picture, the [free overthinking test](/en/overthinking) measures exactly these four patterns: dwelling on the past, worrying about the future, indecision and social replay.

Overthinking also links with other patterns. People who worry a lot about how others see them often recognise themselves in [people pleasing](/en/people-pleaser), and replaying unanswered messages is something we described in detail in our article on [anxious attachment and texting](/en/blog/anxious-attachment-texting).

## How to stop overthinking at night: steps that help

Trying to force thoughts away rarely works. The harder you tell yourself not to think about something, the more it tends to come back. The steps below work differently: they give thoughts a place, a time or a task, so your mind can let go.

### 1. Plan a worry slot earlier in the evening

This idea comes from work by Borkovec and colleagues on postponing worry. Pick a fixed time, say 15 minutes at seven o'clock, at a table and not in bed. Write down everything that is on your mind. When a worry shows up later, tell yourself: "That is for tomorrow's slot." It sounds too simple, but it gives the mind a reason to wait.

### 2. Make a parking list

Keep a notebook next to your bed. If something pops up, write it down in one line, with the first small step next to it if there is one. "Call dentist, 9:00." The point is not to solve anything. It is to show your brain that the thought has been stored somewhere safe.

### 3. Ask the concrete question

Rumination asks "why". Why am I like this, why did it go wrong. Researchers such as Edward Watkins found that switching to concrete questions often helps more: what exactly happened, and what can I do differently next time? A "how" or "what" question has an end. A "why" question usually does not.

### 4. Get up if you are awake for a long time

If you have been lying awake for what feels like twenty minutes or more, get up. Go to another room, keep the light low and do something calm and boring: fold laundry, read a paper book, listen to quiet music. Go back to bed when you feel sleepy. This helps your brain link bed with sleeping again.

### 5. Give your mind something harmless to do

A busy mind does not like being empty. Give it a neutral task instead. Some people use the "cognitive shuffle" suggested by the Canadian researcher Luc Beaudoin: pick a word, such as "bread", and for each letter think of random unrelated objects (b: bicycle, banana, bucket). It is just interesting enough to hold attention, and just dull enough to let you drift off.

### 6. Breathe out longer than you breathe in

Slow breathing will not erase your worries, but it tells your body that there is no emergency. Breathe in through your nose for about four counts and out for about six. Do this for a few minutes. Many people find their thoughts slow down along with their breath.

### 7. Lower the stakes for small decisions

If you overthink choices, set yourself a rule for small ones: two options, five minutes, then pick. The psychologist Barry Schwartz describes the difference between "maximisers", who look for the best possible option, and "satisficers", who pick the first one that is good enough. Satisficers tend to be happier with their choices. For most daily decisions, good enough really is good enough.

## What helps during the day

Night time overthinking often starts in the daytime. A few habits can make your evenings lighter:

- **Close the loops you can close.** Send the email, make the call, book the appointment. Small open tasks are favourite material for a busy mind.
- **Move your body.** A walk, a bike ride or a sports session gives your mind a break and makes you physically tired, which helps sleep.
- **Put the phone down earlier.** Messages, news and social media late in the evening hand your mind fresh material just before you want it to rest.
- **Talk it through once.** Saying a worry out loud to someone you trust often makes it smaller. Talking about the same worry every day, though, can turn into a second form of overthinking.
- **Catch yourself kindly.** "There I go again" works better than "What is wrong with me". Overthinking is a habit, not a character flaw.

## What the overthinking test can and cannot tell you

Our [overthinking test](/en/overthinking) asks how often certain thoughts and situations happen to you and scores four patterns: rumination, future worry, indecision and social replay. You see which ones are high, which are low, and you get practical tips for each.

What it can do is give you language for something that often feels vague. Knowing that your main issue is social replay, not worry about the future, helps you choose the right steps. It can also be a good way to start a conversation with a partner, a friend or a therapist.

What it cannot do is diagnose anything. A high score does not mean you have an anxiety disorder, and a low score does not mean everything is fine. The test only knows your answers on one particular day. A stressful week, a breakup or a new job can push your scores up for a while. Use the result as a mirror, not a verdict.

## When to talk to someone

Overthinking is common and usually manageable with the steps above. It is worth talking to your GP or a psychologist if it keeps you awake most nights for several weeks, if it makes it hard to work or see people, or if your thoughts become very dark. Therapies such as cognitive behavioural therapy are often used for persistent worry and rumination, and you do not need to wait until things are really bad to ask for help.

Tonight, start small. Write down what is on your mind before you go to bed, put the notebook on the nightstand and tell yourself that it will still be there in the morning. Most thoughts become a lot quieter once they know they have been heard.
