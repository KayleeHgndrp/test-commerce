Ordinary mood swings are short and follow what happens in your life. Bipolar disorder works differently: it comes in episodes that last days or weeks, in which your energy, sleep and behaviour change clearly from your normal self. The most telling sign is not feeling low, but a period of unusually high energy in which you hardly need sleep.

Almost everyone says "I am so bipolar today" at some point, after a morning full of energy and an evening on the sofa. That is not what the condition looks like. Below you will read what bipolar disorder actually is, how it differs from normal ups and downs, three situations that are easy to confuse, and what you can do if you have doubts about yourself.

## What is bipolar disorder?

Bipolar disorder is a mood disorder in which periods of depression alternate with periods of unusually elevated or irritable mood. It used to be called manic depression. Between episodes many people feel stable for weeks, months or even years.

The diagnostic manuals that psychiatrists use, the American DSM-5 and the WHO's ICD-11, distinguish a few forms:

- **Bipolar I.** At least one manic episode. Mania lasts at least a week, most of the day, nearly every day, or shorter if someone has to be admitted to hospital. Many people with bipolar I also have depressive episodes.
- **Bipolar II.** At least one hypomanic episode and at least one depressive episode. Hypomania is a milder form of mania that lasts at least four days in a row. It is noticeable to others, but does not completely derail someone's life.
- **Cyclothymia.** For at least two years, regular periods with some hypomanic and some depressive symptoms, but never severe enough to count as a full episode.

What do mania and hypomania look like? Someone feels unusually energetic, euphoric or irritable. They need much less sleep and still feel fit. Thoughts race, they talk faster and jump from one idea to the next. They start many projects at once, spend more money than usual or take risks they would normally avoid. In full mania this can go as far as losing touch with reality.

A depressive episode lasts at least two weeks: low mood or loss of interest, tiredness, sleeping too much or too little, difficulty concentrating, feelings of guilt or worthlessness, and sometimes thoughts of death.

Bipolar disorder often starts in the late teens or early adulthood. It runs in families, but there is rarely one single cause. With treatment, usually a combination of medication, psychological support and a steady daily rhythm, many people live a stable life.

## Bipolar or just mood swings: what is the difference?

This is the question most people actually type into Google. The difference is rarely in how bad a moment feels. It is in the pattern. Five things help to tell them apart.

**1. Duration.** Normal mood swings last hours, sometimes a day or two. A bipolar episode lasts days to weeks and has a clear beginning and end. You do not wake up "different" for one morning, you are different for a period.

**2. Cause.** Ordinary moods usually follow events: a nice weekend, a fight, a deadline, bad news. Bipolar episodes can be triggered by stress or lack of sleep, but often seem to come out of nothing and do not match what is going on.

**3. Sleep and energy.** This is the most important clue. In a hypomanic or manic period people sleep three or four hours and wake up rested, night after night. That is different from lying awake because you are worrying and feeling exhausted the next day.

**4. Behaviour.** In an episode, people do things that do not fit them: large purchases, sudden plans, much more talking, sending messages at night, starting conflicts. Someone with normal mood swings feels different, but mostly keeps acting like themselves.

**5. Other people notice.** Hypomania often feels good from the inside. That is exactly why it is hard to spot yourself. Friends, a partner or colleagues often notice first: "you are so fast", "you are not yourself", "you are spending a lot lately".

| | Ordinary mood swings | Bipolar episode |
|---|---|---|
| How long | Hours to a day or two | Days to weeks, sometimes months |
| Trigger | Usually clear | Often unclear or out of proportion |
| Sleep | Normal, or worse when stressed | Much less need for sleep without feeling tired |
| Behaviour | Mostly stays like you | Clearly different from your normal self |
| Others notice | Rarely | Often |

## Three situations that are easy to confuse

### The week after the exams

Maya is 21. After weeks of studying she passes her final exams. For a week she goes out almost every night, sleeps five hours, plans a trip with friends and feels on top of the world. Then she sleeps for two days, feels a bit flat and picks up her normal life.

This is not hypomania. Her high mood has a clear reason, she gets tired from the short nights and catches up on sleep, and afterwards everything returns to normal. Intense, but fully understandable.

### The man with three new businesses

Daniel is 34 and normally a calm accountant. One spring, without any special reason, he starts sleeping four hours a night and still feels great. Within two weeks he registers three companies, orders expensive equipment and calls friends at midnight with plans. When his partner tries to slow him down, he gets angry. After about three weeks the energy drains away, followed by a long, dark period in which he can hardly get out of bed.

This pattern, a clear period of reduced need for sleep, rapid plans, risky spending and irritability, followed by depression, is what doctors look for. It is a reason to see a doctor, not to wait.

### The depression that kept coming back

Sophie is 28 and has been treated for depression twice. Antidepressants and therapy help, but the dips keep returning. Only when a psychiatrist asks about other periods does something come up. Every spring she has a few weeks in which she sleeps little, works through her whole to do list, buys new clothes and feels "finally like herself". She always saw those weeks as the normal ones.

This situation is common. Bipolar II is often recognised late, because people seek help during the depression and do not mention the good periods. Hypomania does not feel like a problem. That is why a doctor will ask about it explicitly.

## Is it bipolar disorder or borderline?

This confusion comes up often, because both involve intense emotions. The difference again lies in the pattern.

In bipolar disorder moods come in **episodes** of days to weeks, with a clear change in sleep and energy, and between episodes someone can be completely stable. In borderline personality disorder emotions often shift **within hours**, usually triggered by something in a relationship: a message that stays unanswered, a feeling of being rejected. There are also lasting problems with self image, emptiness and fear of being abandoned.

The two can also occur together. If you recognise yourself more in strong reactions to other people, our [BPD test](/en/bpd) looks at that pattern. ## How do you recognise it in yourself?

Because hypomania often feels good, you rarely recognise it in the moment. Looking back works better. These questions can help:

- **Have you ever had a period of several days in which you needed much less sleep and still felt full of energy?** Not one night, but several in a row.
- **Did people around you say you were different?** Faster, louder, more irritable, more restless.
- **Did you do things you later regretted or did not recognise in yourself?** Large purchases, sudden decisions, risky behaviour, messages you would not normally send.
- **Did that period end in a dip?** A crash after the high is a common pattern.
- **Does bipolar disorder or a mood disorder run in your family?** That does not mean you have it, but it is useful information for a doctor.

Note the difference with racing thoughts at night. Lying awake because your head will not stop and feeling broken the next day is much more typical of worrying than of hypomania. If you recognise that, the article on [signs that you are an overthinker](/en/blog/signs-you-are-an-overthinker) is a better starting point.

Doctors sometimes use the Mood Disorder Questionnaire (MDQ), a short screening list developed by Hirschfeld and colleagues in 2000, to find out whether further assessment makes sense. A screening list, just like our test, is a first step, not the conclusion.

## What can you do if you have doubts?

**Keep a mood diary for a few weeks.** Every evening, write down a score for your mood from 1 to 10, how many hours you slept, your energy and anything special. Patterns that you cannot see from day to day become visible over weeks. A doctor can do much more with this than with "I sometimes feel down".

**Ask someone close to you.** Ask a partner, friend or parent whether they have ever noticed periods in which you were clearly different. Their view adds what you miss yourself.

**Protect your sleep.** Regular bedtimes and enough sleep help almost everyone keep their mood more stable. Too little sleep can also trigger an episode in people who are susceptible to it. Be careful with alcohol and drugs, they make mood swings bigger.

**Talk to your GP.** Take your diary and a few concrete examples. Mention not only the low periods, but also the times you felt unusually good or energetic. That is often the information that leads to the right diagnosis.

**Look for help straight away when things are urgent.** If you are having thoughts of ending your life, or if someone close to you seems to lose touch with reality, contact a doctor, emergency services or a crisis line in your country immediately. That is not an overreaction.

## What our test does and does not tell you

Our [bipolar test](/en/bipolar) asks about four areas: periods of elevated mood, energy and activity, depressive periods, and how your mood shifts over time. You think about your life as a whole, not just the last few weeks. It takes about five minutes.

**What it does:** it shows on which of those areas your answers stand out, in clear language. **What it does not do:** it does not make a diagnosis. A low score does not guarantee that nothing is going on, and a high score does not mean you have bipolar disorder. Only a doctor or psychiatrist can sort that out, after a proper conversation about your history.

So see the outcome as a conversation starter. If your results raise questions, take them seriously and discuss them with someone who can look at the whole picture.

## When are mood swings simply part of life?

Not every rollercoaster needs a label. Hormonal changes, a period of stress, too little sleep, grief or a big life change can all make your mood more unstable for a while. That is human. As long as your moods follow what happens, you sleep normally when life is calm and you can keep doing your daily things, it is probably part of who you are and what you are going through.

It becomes a different story when the swings come in clear periods, change your sleep and behaviour, and keep you from functioning. Then it is worth looking further, not to stick a label on yourself, but because good help really makes a difference.
